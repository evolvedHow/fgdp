## SEGMENT 1: The Computer That Drew 38,505 Maps

Here's a question worth sitting with: What would Georgia's congressional districts look like if nobody was cheating?

That's not a rhetorical question. We can actually answer it now — and the answer is both fascinating and damning.

Over the past year, two independent research teams — one from Princeton, one from Harvard — ran computer programs that drew Georgia's legislative maps. Over and over again. Not once, not a hundred times. Thirty-eight thousand, five hundred and five times.

[PAUSE]

Let that sink in. Thirty-eight thousand maps.

Specifically: 9,501 maps for Georgia's 14 congressional seats. 9,001 maps for the State Senate. And 24,003 maps for the State House.

Each map was drawn the same way: by a computer algorithm that knew only about geography, population balance, and keeping communities together. It didn't know which party lived where. It didn't know the names of any incumbents. It had no agenda.

[HOST NOTE: Pause here for emphasis — this is the core credibility moment]

Now — when you draw nearly ten thousand maps of a state's congressional districts, you start to see something powerful: a bell curve. Most of those maps cluster around a center. That center is what a fair map looks like for Georgia. Not one person's opinion of fair. Not a compromise negotiated in a back room. A statistical consensus, built from tens of thousands of independent attempts.

Think of it like a coin flip experiment. If you flip a coin ten times, you might get seven heads. Weird things happen in small samples. But if you flip a coin ten thousand times? You're going to land very close to fifty-fifty. The randomness averages out, and the truth emerges.

That's what these 38,505 maps are. They're ten thousand coin flips for Georgia's democracy. And what they reveal about the maps that are actually in use is remarkable.

[TRANSITION MUSIC — brief, under 5 seconds]

The two teams used different tools. Princeton's team used a method called GerryChain — a Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm that essentially takes random walks through all the possible ways you could draw district boundaries while following the rules. Harvard's team used a framework called ALARM — the Algorithm for Redistricting and Localized Measurements — which is built on top of a statistical package called redist.

Different math. Different code. Different researchers.

[EMPHASIS: Same findings.]

When two completely independent teams, using different methodologies, arrive at the same conclusions about the same maps — that's not coincidence. That's a result you can take to court. That's a result you can take to the public.

So let's talk about what they found.

## SEGMENT 2: The Congressional Map — Good Enough to Call Fair?

Georgia is a purple state. Not deep red, not deep blue — purple.

Here's the number: when you combine election results across multiple statewide races — governor, Senate, president — and weight them by the voting-age population across all 2,698 of Georgia's voting tabulation districts, you get a composite Democratic vote share of 51.49 percent.

[PAUSE]

Fifty-one point four nine percent. Democratic voters are, by a slim but real margin, the majority of Georgia's voting-age population.

[HOST NOTE: Let this land — most listeners will be surprised]

Now — what does 51.49 percent of the statewide vote translate to in congressional seats? In a fair system, you'd expect something close to proportionality. Fifty-one percent of the vote should get you something close to fifty-one percent of the seats. Georgia has 14 congressional districts. Fifty-one percent of fourteen is 7.2 seats.

Seven seats. That's what proportionality looks like for Georgia.

The neutral ensemble — those 9,501 computer-drawn maps — has a median outcome of 6 Democratic-leaning seats. The range runs from 5 to 8. So fair maps cluster around 6, with some variation on either side.

The enacted map — the one Georgia's Republican-controlled legislature actually drew — produces 5 Democratic-leaning seats.

[PAUSE]

Five out of fourteen, with 51.49 percent of the votes. That's 35.7 percent of the seats.

There's a name for the gap between your vote share and your seat share. It's called the proportionality gap. In Georgia's congressional map, Democratic voters earn 51.49 percent of the vote and receive 35.7 percent of the seats. The gap is 15.8 percentage points. That's not a rounding error. That's not geographic sorting. That's a structural disadvantage baked into the map.

Now, some people will say: "Five seats is inside the neutral range of five to eight — so isn't it technically fair?" This is where we need to be precise.

[EMPHASIS: Being inside the range is not the same as being fair.]

Five is the floor of that range. It's the most Republican-leaning outcome that a neutral, legally compliant map tends to produce. When the enacted map lands exactly at the bottom of the distribution — not in the middle, not near the center, but at the very bottom — that tells you something.

And the detailed partisan metrics confirm it. Political scientists have developed two standard measurements for map fairness: the efficiency gap, which measures wasted votes, and the mean-median difference, which measures whether the median district is shifted away from the statewide average.

Georgia's enacted congressional map scores at the 96th percentile on the efficiency gap — meaning it's more skewed than 96 percent of neutral computer-drawn maps. On the mean-median difference? It scores at the 100th percentile. The 100th. That means [EMPHASIS: every single one] of the 9,501 neutral maps produced a more balanced outcome than Georgia's enacted congressional map.

And then there are competitive seats. In a healthy democracy, you want some districts where the outcome isn't predetermined — where either party could win depending on how the voters feel that year. Those districts create accountability. They force politicians to actually listen to their constituents.

How many competitive congressional seats does Georgia's enacted map have?

[PAUSE]

Zero.

The typical neutral map has two to four genuinely competitive seats. Georgia's enacted map has none. Every single district is locked in for one party or the other before a single vote is cast.

The overall congressional grade: a C. Not an F — the map avoids the most extreme partisan manipulation, largely because the underlying demographics in a few districts make it difficult to do much worse. But a C is not something to be proud of. A C means "better than the worst, nowhere near the best, and clearly tilted."

## SEGMENT 3: The Senate and House — A Tale of Two Chambers

If the congressional map is a C-grade story, the State Senate map is an F-grade catastrophe.

Georgia's State Senate has 56 seats. With 51.49 percent of the statewide vote, proportionality would give Democrats roughly 28 or 29 seats. The neutral ensemble — those 9,001 computer-drawn Senate maps — has a median outcome of 26 Democratic seats.

Georgia's enacted Senate map produces 23 Democratic seats.

[PAUSE]

Twenty-three out of fifty-six, with 51.49 percent of the statewide vote. That's the 3.3rd percentile.

Put that in plain terms: if you drew a random, legally compliant, geographically sensible Senate map — one that didn't consider party at all — there's only a 3.3 percent chance you'd accidentally produce a map this unfavorable to Democratic voters.

[HOST NOTE: Use the lottery analogy here]

Think about it like a lottery. If someone tells you they drew a random number between 1 and 100, and that number happened to be 3 or lower, you'd be suspicious. Not certain something went wrong — but suspicious. Now imagine they did that deliberately, with the explicit goal of winning. That's the Georgia State Senate map.

The efficiency gap for the Senate map sits at the 99th percentile. The mean-median difference is at the 100th percentile — again, every single neutral map outperformed the enacted map. And competitive seats? The enacted Senate map has zero competitive seats. Zero. That's also at the 99.9th percentile — meaning essentially no neutral map produces this level of uncompetitiveness.

Grade: F. Not close to passing.

The State House tells a different story — and it's an important one. Georgia's House has 180 seats. The enacted map produces 85 Democratic seats. The neutral ensemble's median is 86. The grade is B or C, not an F.

Why is the House different? Partly it's math — with 180 districts, there's less room to dramatically skew outcomes before you start violating basic geographic constraints. And partly it's a genuine finding: the House districts, while imperfect, aren't dramatically different from what a fair process would produce.

[TRANSITION MUSIC]

This contrast — F for the Senate, B/C for the House — is itself a finding worth noting. It's not that the legislature doesn't know how to draw fair maps. They drew one, more or less, for the House. They chose not to for the Senate. And emphatically chose not to for Congress.

That's not incompetence. That's a decision.

## SEGMENT 4: The Paradox — The Map That Looked Clean

Here's where the story gets genuinely strange. And important.

One of the standard arguments map-drawers use to defend their work goes something like this: "We didn't use partisan data. We just tried to keep communities together. That's why our lines look clean — see, we didn't split many cities."

For Georgia's congressional map, that argument has unusual surface credibility. Because the enacted map actually does have very few city splits. In fact, it has fewer city splits than almost any neutral map.

[PAUSE]

The typical neutral congressional map splits between 21 and 33 cities across Georgia's 14 districts. The enacted map splits just 12.

[EMPHASIS: Twelve.]

That's the 0.1st percentile. The map is cleaner on city boundaries than 99.9 percent of neutral maps. If you gave someone only that statistic, they might conclude this was the most community-conscious, carefully drawn map in Georgia history.

And yet — 0 competitive seats. Senate at the 3.3rd percentile. Congressional efficiency gap at the 96th percentile.

How do you reconcile those two facts? How can a map simultaneously be cleaner than nearly every neutral map on geography AND more gerrymandered than nearly every neutral map on partisan outcomes?

[HOST NOTE: This is the intellectual heart of the episode — slow down]

The answer is what researchers call sub-municipal manipulation.

Here's the key insight: you don't need to split cities to gerrymander. In fact, splitting fewer cities can be part of the gerrymander, if you're strategic about which cities you split and which you don't.

Think of it this way. Imagine a state with two big cities — both heavily Democratic. You could split each city between two districts, diluting the Democratic vote. That's the obvious, crude version of cracking.

Or — and this is the sophisticated version — you could keep both cities whole, but draw the district lines very carefully through the suburbs and exurbs that surround those cities. You contain the Democratic cities in as few districts as possible while spreading Republican-leaning suburbs across multiple districts. The cities are intact. The lines look clean. But the outcome is the same.

That's what the data suggests is happening in Georgia. The manipulation isn't happening at the city level — it's happening at the precinct level, in the suburbs and exurbs, in places too small and too granular for most voters to notice.

[PAUSE]

The cities look clean. The map looks clean. And underneath the clean lines, the partisan math is engineered.

This is why ensemble analysis is so powerful. It cuts through the surface aesthetics and asks the only question that matters: compared to the universe of fair alternatives, where does this map fall? And the answer, for Georgia's State Senate and congressional maps, is: near the bottom.

## SEGMENT 5: Race and the Maps

There's one more dimension to this story that we need to discuss, and it's one that's often treated as separate from the partisan analysis. But increasingly, the data suggests they're deeply connected.

In May of 2026, the New York Times published a major study on redistricting and race. The finding was striking: when researchers drew race-neutral maps — maps that didn't consider racial composition at all, just geography and population — those maps naturally produced the same number of minority opportunity districts as maps specifically designed to comply with the Voting Rights Act.

[PAUSE]

What that means, in plain terms: you don't need to choose between partisan fairness and racial fairness. A map that's geographically clean and politically neutral will, in a state like Georgia with a large and geographically concentrated Black population, produce minority opportunity districts organically.

For Georgia specifically: race-neutral maps would produce 6 minority opportunity congressional districts. Six.

The enacted congressional map produces 5 Democratic-leaning seats — fewer than even the neutral maps' median outcome. And the study suggests that if you drew the map fairly on both partisan and racial grounds, Georgia would have 6 seats where minority communities have a genuine opportunity to elect their preferred representative.

[HOST NOTE: Be careful here — explain VRA without legalism]

Now, let's be clear about what the Voting Rights Act requires. The VRA sets a floor, not a ceiling. It says: in places where minority communities are large enough and compact enough to form a majority in a district, you can't draw lines that deliberately dilute their voting power. That's the floor.

But the VRA doesn't say you have to draw the bare minimum number of minority opportunity districts. It doesn't say you have to pack minority voters into a handful of districts and then spread white voters across the rest in a way that maximizes one party's advantage. The VRA is a civil rights protection — not a gerrymandering license.

And what this study shows is that when you draw maps the right way — neutrally, geographically, without manipulation — the VRA floor and partisan fairness end up pointing to the same answer. Six congressional seats in Georgia where minority communities have real representation, and six or seven Democratic-leaning seats overall.

[TRANSITION MUSIC]

This matters enormously for 2026. Georgia has a congressional election this year. Every one of those 14 seats is on the ballot. The lines are already drawn. But the data showing what fair lines would look like — 6 seats, competitive races, proportional outcomes — that data exists now. It didn't exist before. Two independent research teams built it, verified it, and published it.

That's ammunition. For advocates, for litigators, for journalists, for voters.

## SEGMENT 6: What a Fair Map Would Look Like

So let's close with something concrete. Not a lawsuit. Not a statistical abstraction. Just a picture of what Georgia's democracy could look like.

In a fairly drawn congressional map, Georgia would have 6 Democratic-leaning seats and 8 Republican-leaning seats. That's not a Democratic majority — Republicans would still hold more seats. But it would be roughly proportional to how Georgia actually votes.

[EMPHASIS: Republicans would still win more seats. They just wouldn't win them by rigging the map.]

There would be 2 to 4 genuinely competitive congressional seats — districts where the outcome isn't predetermined, where candidates of either party would actually have to earn their votes, where a wave year could genuinely flip a district. That accountability is good for everyone, including Republican voters who want their representatives to work for them rather than coast on a safe seat.

In the State Senate, a fair map would produce roughly 26 Democratic seats out of 56 — not a Democratic majority, but close to proportional, and more representative of a state where Democrats have a narrow majority of the voting-age population.

[PAUSE]

Here's the simplest way to think about what's at stake: Georgia's maps, as drawn, systematically convert a 51.49 percent Democratic vote share into congressional and Senate chambers where Republicans hold large, stable majorities. That's not democracy. That's not what the founders had in mind. It's not what the courts intend when they uphold districting plans.

And it's not what 38,505 neutral maps look like.

[HOST NOTE: Call to action — keep it specific and actionable]

FairDistricts GA is working to change this. The next redistricting cycle will happen after the 2030 Census. That seems far away, but the groundwork for fair maps is built in the years before the lines are drawn — through litigation, through legislation, through public pressure, and through the accumulation of exactly this kind of evidence.

If you're in Georgia, register to vote and stay registered. Attend your local school board meeting, your city council meeting, your county commission. These local bodies feed into state legislative redistricting in ways most people don't realize.

If you're not in Georgia, this story is playing out in your state too. The ensemble methodology — the same 38,505-maps approach — can be applied anywhere. The tools exist. The researchers are willing to use them. What's needed is the public demand to make the findings matter.

[PAUSE]

We started this episode with a question: What would Georgia's maps look like if nobody was cheating?

We know the answer now. Six congressional seats. Twenty-six State Senate seats. Two to four competitive races where voters actually decide the outcome. A map that looks like Georgia.

The computers drew it 38,505 times.

The legislature chose something else.
