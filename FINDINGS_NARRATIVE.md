# Georgia's Rigged Maps: What 38,505 Neutral Redistricting Plans Reveal

## What We Did

A team of computers spent weeks drawing maps. Not political strategists, not legislative staff, not lawyers with precinct data and partisan targets — just algorithms following simple rules: keep districts roughly equal in population, keep them geographically contiguous, and don't ask who lives where or how they vote.

We generated 38,505 such maps across Georgia's three chambers — 9,501 for the 14 congressional districts, 9,001 for the 56 State Senate districts, and 24,003 for the 180 State House districts — using a method developed by the Princeton Gerrymandering Project and the MGGG Redistricting Lab at Tufts. For Congress, we also used an independent set of 5,000 maps from Harvard's ALARM Project, generated with a different algorithm entirely.

The method is called an ensemble benchmark. Think of it this way: instead of asking "is this map fair?" in the abstract, we ask "compared to the full universe of maps that follow the same rules, where does Georgia's enacted map land?" If the enacted map falls comfortably in the middle of that distribution, we call it normal. If it sits at the extreme edge — if the enacted map produces partisan outcomes that almost no neutral map would produce — we call that a statistical outlier. An outlier that consistently favors one party has a name: a gerrymander.

The baseline election data covers six races from 2018 through 2024: the 2018 Governor's race (Kemp vs. Abrams), the 2020 Presidential race (Trump vs. Biden), the January 2021 Senate runoffs (Warnock/Loeffler), the 2022 Governor's race (Kemp vs. Abrams again), the November 2022 Senate race (Walker vs. Warnock), and the 2024 Presidential race (Trump vs. Harris). Each election is weighted equally. Averaged across all six, Democrats receive approximately 49–51% of the two-party vote across Georgia — a genuinely competitive state where either party could plausibly win a majority of legislative seats under neutral redistricting.

## What We Found — Congress (14 seats)

Across 9,501 neutral congressional maps, the median outcome is **6 Democratic seats and 2 competitive races**. The central 90% of neutral maps produce between 5 and 8 Democratic-leaning seats, with 0 to 4 genuinely competitive districts where either party could win in a given cycle.

The enacted map produces **5 Democratic seats and zero competitive races**.

Five out of 14 seats is 35.7% of representation for a party that wins roughly half the votes. Six seats — what a typical neutral map produces — would still be below strict proportionality, but it would be within the normal range of what good-faith redistricting produces in a state with Georgia's geographic patterns. Five is not.

The enacted map scores in the **24th percentile** for Democratic seat share: three-quarters of neutral maps give Democrats more seats. That is a B grade under the ensemble framework — meaning the outcome is below the neutral median but not a statistical outlier in the strict sense. However, two other metrics tell a sharper story.

The **efficiency gap** — a measure of how symmetrically each party's votes translate into seats — sits at the **96th percentile** of neutral maps. Every vote cast in a losing district is a "wasted" vote; so is every vote above the margin needed to win a district. A fair map wastes both parties' votes at similar rates. The enacted congressional map wastes Democratic votes at a rate matched or exceeded by fewer than 4% of neutral alternatives. Both the Princeton (GerryChain) and Harvard (ALARM) benchmarks independently produce this same finding.

The **mean-median difference** — a measure of how symmetrically Democratic votes are distributed across districts — sits at the **100th percentile**. Every single one of the 14,501 neutral congressional maps analyzed by both teams has a more symmetrical vote distribution than the enacted map. This is the most unambiguous statistical finding in the entire analysis. It means Democratic votes are systematically concentrated: packed into a handful of landslide districts, diluted in others, never quite enough to win a competitive seat that might otherwise exist.

**The congressional map receives an overall grade of C.** That C is not reassuring. It reflects that the seat count, while below the neutral median, hasn't crossed into the 5th-percentile statistical outlier zone. But the vote distribution metrics are at the extreme tail of the ensemble — and a C on partisan fairness in a chamber where zero seats are competitive is not a passing grade in any practical sense. An independent study by The New York Times (May 2026) found that race-neutral redistricting simulations would produce approximately 6 minority-opportunity seats in Georgia — the same as the ensemble neutral median, confirming that the 1-seat manipulation gap is simultaneously a partisan gerrymandering finding and a VRA Section 2 dilution concern.

### The Racial and Partisan Stories Converge

The May 2026 New York Times study on redistricting and race ("Redistricting, Race and Court Gerrymanders," May 17, 2026) reached a striking conclusion: race-neutral redistricting simulations across the South produce approximately the same number of minority-opportunity districts as maps explicitly drawn to comply with the Voting Rights Act. The VRA doesn't create extra minority districts — fair maps naturally produce them.

For Georgia, the Times study found that neutral maps would yield approximately **6 minority-opportunity congressional seats**. Our ensemble confirms this independently: the neutral median is exactly 6 Democratic-leaning congressional seats, of which the vast majority would be won by candidates preferred by communities of color.

This means the partisan and racial gerrymandering findings are telling the same story:

- **Partisan finding**: The enacted map produces 5 Democratic seats. Neutral maps typically produce 6. The manipulation gap = **1 seat**.
- **Racial finding**: Neutral maps support 6 minority-opportunity seats. The enacted map has fewer. The VRA floor deficit = **1 seat**.

**They are the same seat.** The congressional manipulation — the deliberate decision to draw lines that produce one fewer Democratic seat than neutral redistricting would — simultaneously withholds the seat that minority voters would have won. The racial gerrymandering and the partisan gerrymandering are not separate violations: they are one act.

This convergence matters for 2026 advocacy and litigation because it means the ensemble benchmark provides evidence relevant to both Fourteenth Amendment partisan gerrymandering claims and Section 2 Voting Rights Act dilution claims. The same 38,505 neutral maps speak to both.

## What We Found — State Senate (56 seats)

The State Senate tells a starker story.

Neutral maps of Georgia's State Senate typically produce **26 Democratic seats** (the ensemble median). The enacted map produces **23 seats** — placing it at the **3.3rd percentile**. Only 3 out of every 100 neutral maps produce outcomes as unfavorable to Democrats as the enacted Senate map.

The efficiency gap is at the **99th percentile**. The mean-median difference is again at the **100th percentile** — no neutral Senate map has a more asymmetric vote distribution than the enacted one. The enacted map has **zero competitive seats** in a 56-district chamber, a result that falls at the **0.1st percentile** — meaning 99.9% of neutral Senate maps produce at least one genuinely competitive district. The typical neutral map produces four.

The enacted Senate map has **29 safe Republican seats**. The entire ensemble of 9,001 neutral maps tops out at roughly 27. The enacted map is more extreme than any neutral alternative.

**The State Senate receives a Grade F.** Five of the six partisan and competitive metrics are Grade F. This is the most severely gerrymandered chamber in our analysis.

Democrats win roughly 41% of Senate seats while casting approximately half the votes. The gap between votes and seats — the "proportionality gap" — amounts to approximately 3 Senate seats relative to what neutral redistricting would typically produce.

## What We Found — State House (180 seats)

The State House is the most complicated finding, and in some ways the most instructive.

Neutral maps produce approximately **85 Democratic House seats** (the ensemble median range is 82–88). The enacted map produces **84 Democratic seats**, placing it at the **36th percentile** — slightly below average, but unremarkable by itself. The efficiency gap receives a B grade. On raw seat count, the House looks almost normal.

But two metrics break sharply in the other direction.

The mean-median difference for the House enacted map is at the **100th percentile** — again, the most extreme value in the entire ensemble. Not a single one of the 24,003 neutral House maps has a more asymmetric Democratic vote distribution. This matters because mean-median can detect manipulation that the efficiency gap misses: a map that never lets Democrats win a competitive seat but keeps all Democratic districts safely above 55% will score moderately on efficiency gap while failing mean-median dramatically.

**Safe Republican seats** in the House stand at 89, placing the enacted map at the **100th percentile** — more reliably Republican districts than any neutral alternative produces. The ensemble tops out at around 85.

The House's minority representation findings compound the picture. The enacted map contains **50 majority-Black districts**. The neutral ensemble produces 35 to 40. Concentrating Black voters into far more majority-Black districts than geography requires — a technique called racial packing — has a downstream effect: it reduces Black voters' influence in adjacent districts, which then become more safely Republican.

**The House receives an overall grade of C**, driven primarily by the near-normal seat count and efficiency gap. But the mean-median outlier and the racial packing signal indicate that the House map achieves its near-normal efficiency gap through a specific mechanism: Democratic landslides substituted for Democratic competitive districts. The votes are being concentrated, not spread.

## What the Grades Mean — and Don't Mean

The ensemble framework grades maps *relative to neutral redistricting* — not relative to perfect proportionality. This distinction matters enormously.

Even perfectly neutral maps of Georgia tend to produce fewer Democratic seats than strict proportionality would imply. That is partly geography: Democratic voters concentrate in Atlanta and its suburbs, in college towns, in majority-minority communities — clustering that makes it structurally harder to translate raw vote share into seat share even without any deliberate manipulation. This "structural gap" would exist regardless of who drew the maps.

The ensemble grades isolate the *manipulation gap* — the additional deviation from the neutral median attributable to deliberate redistricting choices. A C grade for Congress means the manipulation gap is real but not at the extreme statistical threshold. An F grade for the Senate means the manipulation gap is extreme by any standard.

The most honest number is the full proportionality picture: Democrats win approximately half Georgia's votes and receive 35.7% of congressional seats, 41.1% of State Senate seats, and 46.7% of State House seats. Some portion of those gaps is structural geography. The ensemble analysis quantifies what portion is manipulation — and in the Senate in particular, the answer is: most of it.

## The Urban Cracking Paradox

Traditional gerrymandering theory predicts that splitting cities across multiple districts harms Democrats, because urban voters lose concentrated representation. The enacted maps appear to take the opposite approach — and it reveals something important about how modern gerrymandering works.

The enacted congressional map splits **12 municipalities** across district lines. The neutral ensemble typically splits 27. Splitting fewer cities than almost any neutral map earns the enacted congressional map a Grade A for municipal integrity. The Senate and House maps show the same pattern.

But here is the finding that should concern anyone watching Georgia politics: across 9,501 neutral congressional maps, the statistical correlation between municipal splits and Democratic seat share is essentially **zero** (r = 0.026). Preserving city boundaries has no meaningful relationship to partisan outcomes. None.

The manipulation is happening *below* the city level — at the scale of individual Voting Tabulation Districts, the sub-neighborhood geographic units that the ensemble operates on. Districts are drawn to respect city limits in the aggregate while carefully sorting the VTDs within and between cities in ways that dilute Democratic concentration without triggering the most visible warning sign. A city's boundaries are intact; the communities within it are split in ways that are invisible to any headline metric.

This is not less sophisticated gerrymandering than the traditional kind. It is more sophisticated. It passes the geographic tests while failing the electoral ones.

## Why This Matters for 2026

Georgia's current redistricting maps were enacted in 2023 and will govern every state and federal election through at least the 2030 census — eight more years of elections across seven more election cycles.

The ensemble analysis translates into concrete electoral stakes. Zero competitive congressional seats means that 14 Georgia congressional races are effectively decided before they begin. Zero competitive State Senate seats — in a 56-district chamber — means that the majority of Georgia's state senators face no meaningful electoral accountability. When an incumbent has no competitive challenger, voters in that district have a sharply reduced ability to change their representation regardless of how the state's political landscape shifts.

The analysis also establishes a specific, defensible answer to the question: *How many seats should Democrats win in a fair map of Georgia?* The answer is not a political preference — it is a statistical distribution derived from tens of thousands of maps that follow the same legal rules as the enacted ones. Congressional: typically 6, sometimes as many as 8. State Senate: typically 26, with a plausible range of 24 to 28. State House: typically 85, with a range of 82 to 88.

The enacted maps deliver less than the bottom of that range in the Senate, and below the median in all three chambers. That gap — between what neutral redistricting would produce and what the enacted maps produce — is the measure of what Georgia voters are not getting.

## Methodology Notes for Public Communication

**What this analysis is:**
- A statistical comparison of Georgia's enacted maps against tens of thousands of computer-drawn alternatives
- Based on real election data from six races spanning 2018–2024
- Uses two independent methods (GerryChain from Princeton/Tufts and ALARM from Harvard) that reach the same core conclusions
- Each methodology has been published in peer-reviewed venues and used in redistricting litigation

**What this analysis is not:**
- A prediction of future election outcomes
- A claim that Democrats "deserve" any specific number of seats
- A legal filing or expert report (though the methodology is adapted from those used in such contexts)

**Key caveats for audiences:**
- The six-election composite is weighted equally. 2022 contributes two of the six elections (both races), which gives that cycle slightly more weight than others.
- The GerryChain ensemble uses a Markov chain (correlated samples); ALARM uses sequential Monte Carlo (independent samples). Both are accepted methods.
- The `mean_median` and `efficiency_gap` results at the 100th and 96th percentile mean the enacted map is more extreme than all or nearly all neutral alternatives — that is a very strong statistical signal, not a marginal finding.
- Geographic clustering of Democratic voters explains some — but not all — of the partisan gap between votes and seats. The ensemble methodology isolates the portion attributable to deliberate map-drawing choices.
